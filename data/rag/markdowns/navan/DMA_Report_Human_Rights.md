As the world increasingly understands that a more sustainable model of living is a prerequisite for our future, efforts to facilitate avenues for sustainable development have ramped up, engaging trade and investment policy frameworks as well.

In the rapidly evolving landscape of global value chains and international trade, the concept of mandatory due diligence has become pivotal for businesses. Companies must assess and report not only on how sustainability issues affect their financial health, but also on how their business activities impact the world around them. Due diligence instruments help regulate previously unregulated global value chain issues, ensuring corporate accountability, safeguarding human rights, and protecting the environment.

The shift to a duty of care with legal consequences is now a global reality. For example, the European Union Green Deal Policy Initiatives on Global Supply Chain Sustainability includes legislation making due diligence requirements mandatory. Developing countries aiming to integrate into global value chains are increasingly required to comply with and implement a range of sustainability requirements to access target markets and attract investments.

However, these measures can be challenging for developing countries and their businesses – particularly micro, small, and medium-sized enterprises (MSMEs) – seeking to engage in international trade. It is important to ensure that amidst the rapid developments in linking trade to sustainability considerations, developing countries, MSMEs, workers and producers are not left behind.

If this is not ensured, businesses from these countries could be constrained in their ability to engage in international trade, which could, in turn, adversely impact sustainable development.

# About

This report outlines the life cycle of implementing mandatory Human Rights and Environmental Due Diligence (mHREDD) using a participatory approach through the promotion of dialogue, collective action, and sharing of responsibilities with a full set of relevant value chain and ecosystem stakeholders.

The insights provided cover the opportunities and challenges presented by mHREDD in real cases, along with the requirements for various interventions to help actors adapt to the new expectations, leverage opportunities, and mitigate associated challenges.

More broadly, this report sheds light on a process used to design and implement accompanying measures that can be replicated and built upon to reinforce capacities, leverage investments, improve the broader enabling environment and ensure better and more sustainable trade. This approach demonstrates how collaboration and shared responsibility can effectively drive the implementation of mHREDD, ultimately leading to a more sustainable and equitable global trade system.

The pilots involved a diverse group of stakeholders, including producers, regulatory authorities and other government institutions, civil society, MSMEs, multinational corporations, certification bodies, and financial institutions. Development partners, including donors and implementing agencies, also participated. Stakeholder consultations included over 400 participants from both private and public sectors, supported by international and national experts.

The Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive

# AT A GLANCE

The corporate sustainability due diligence directive (CSDDD) aims to enhance respect for the environment and human rights in the EU and globally. The CSDDD will set obligations for large companies regarding actual and potential adverse impacts on human rights and the environment. It establishes a human rights and environmental due diligence duty for in-scope companies. This EU Directive is one of the European Green Deal Policy Initiatives on Global Supply Chain Sustainability.

The Regulation on Deforestation-free products (EUDR)

# AT A GLANCE

The EUDR aims to ensure that EU consumption does not contribute to global deforestation and forest degradation arising from agricultural expansion linked to the relevant commodities. EUDR requirements can be challenging for business and trade, particularly for MSMEs, seeking to engage in international trade and attract investment.

# Core components:

- A prohibition on first placing or making available listed commodities and products on the EU market or exporting them from the EU, unless they are ‘deforestation-free’ and have been also produced in accordance with relevant legislation of the country of production.
- An obligation on companies placing products on the market or exporting them to exercise ‘due diligence’ to ensure their compliance with these criteria.
- A ‘benchmarking’ three-tier system for the assessment of countries (or parts thereof) related level of risk (high; standard; low) for deforestation or forest degradation.

# Commodities and product scope:

Current scope: Cattle, wood, cocoa, soy, palm oil, coffee, rubber, and some of their derived products, such as leather, chocolate, tyres, or furniture *

Also including many derived products (e.g. leather, chocolate, furniture etc.)

# Progressive coverage of additional products in scope of EUDR

after reviews – particular consideration of maize and biodiesel in one-year review (complete by June 2024); others may be added subsequently.

EUDR applicable to both domestically produced and imported commodities and products, and exports from EU.

*Current = June 2024

Accompanying Measures AS SOLUTIONS

No value chain actor can satisfy the requirements of the mandatory Human Rights and Environmental Due Diligence alone.

With the emergence of mHREDD measures, developed to help fulfill the new obligations while ensuring that value chain actors in producer countries – including MSMEs and producers – can better adapt to and/or mitigate associated risks and challenges, while capitalizing on opportunities.

Unchecked, the burden of performance and the risk of commercial loss could fall disproportionately on developing countries and on smaller and medium-sized actors in global value chains.

The guidance document ‘Making Mandatory Human Rights and Environmental Due Diligence Work for All’1 elaborates eight typologies of accompanying measures to support implementation of mHREDD and reduce the burden on producers and MSMEs.

# Partnerships

The pilots and the findings mentioned herewith are the results of efforts by the European Union (EU), the Organization of African, Caribbean and Pacific States (OACPS) Secretariat, the International Trade Centre (ITC), the International Coffee Organization (ICO), and the Latin American and Caribbean Network of Fair Trade Small Producers and Workers (CLAC-Fairtrade) to support policymakers, business support organizations (BSOs) and value chain operators, including MSMEs, in the transition towards complying with mHREDD requirements.

The pilot work to date has focused on the cocoa value chain in Ghana, coffee in Uganda and Ethiopia, and textile and garments in Eswatini. CLAC-Fairtrade is leading four additional pilots in Peru and the Dominican Republic on cocoa, and in Brazil and Honduras on coffee.

Several other countries and value chains were also engaged through information sharing at conferences, workshops, and webinars to meet the global level awareness-raising and information-sharing objectives of the pilots.

# Scope

The value chain actors involved varied in size, from small and large cooperative unions, private and public sector stakeholders from across value chains, directly and indirectly impacted by existing and upcoming legislation.

This co-financed and collaborative initiative further leverages existing ITC projects and established partnerships with OACPS institutions, various value chain actors, including producers’ organizations and MSMEs in exporting countries, the International Social and Environmental Accreditation and Labelling Alliance (ISEAL), Fairtrade International, Fairtrade Max Havelaar, Fairtrade Africa, and the Robusta Coffee Agency of Africa and Madagascar (ACRAM). The process is being led through the Alliances for Action methodology.

# How to design a mHREDD pilot?

We adopted an inclusive and context-specific implementation design.

The pilot process promotes collective and individual action and co-investment. Key goals are to co-create a space for value chain dialogue and to define commonly agreed value chain-based action plans around human rights and environmental due diligence.

The general pilot methodology design kicks off with a preparatory phase to raise awareness on mHREDD amongst industries and value chain operators, select pilot value chains and countries, and develop a concept and work plan.

Main steps include testing the Accompanying Measures Framework, designing a pilot, and receiving and integrating feedback from relevant stakeholders.

# ENHANCING THE BUSINESS ENVIRONMENT

# Mapping of national legal and policy frameworks

Developing countries seeking to integrate into or expand their participation in global value chains are increasingly being required to comply with and meet various sustainability requirements.

In this context, the specific regulatory, policy and institutional contexts of countries will be critical to ensure an enabling environment for businesses to facilitate and report compliance with sustainability-related requirements into their own business and value chain operations. Accordingly, aligning national frameworks with relevant human rights and environment due diligence requirements and CSDDD priorities provides for an enabling environment for the country’s enterprises. It helps ensure compliance with and report on sustainability-related requirements as required under the CSDDD.

The assessment will feed into a plan of action identifying areas for legal and policy reform and capacity building in cooperation with international organizations and development partners.

Through this initiative, the objective is to provide a clear framework through which governments can assess the extent to which their policy frameworks are already aligned, which can be leveraged as a selling point to attract investments. This, in turn, can boost the country’s credibility and opportunities in the global market and strengthen its developmental framework by ensuring compliance with underlying international principles and guidelines drawn from the sustainable reporting requirements. By doing so, enterprises in developing countries can facilitate and further grow value chain relations with business partners who will become subject to the CSDDD, and leverage such developments for investment attraction purposes. For governments in developing countries, taking steps early in anticipation of the impact of the CSDDD on the country’s enterprises and value chains could present a competitive advantage in the market for both the government and enterprises.

The ESG Readiness Assessment provides a profile of the capacity and readiness of an organization to identify and manage social, environmental and governance risks. The assessment contains approximately 60 questions, and the answers are ranked by maturity level, providing a clear profile of the readiness of the organizations and a development pathway for them to follow to achieve greater resilience and sustainability. # The cohorts

A total of 48 organizations completed Readiness Assessments in the pilot projects in Eswatini, Ethiopia, Ghana, and Uganda. These organizations vary in terms of structure, size, sector, value of exports, main markets, and role in the value chain.

To meet the expectations of due diligence laws, companies must be able to identify and then prevent or mitigate human rights and environmental risks in their value chain. We assessed entities in the four pilot countries and discerned three levels of readiness, namely emergent, transition, and tertiary.



<!-- PAGE: 15 -->


The emergent group is characterized by informal or partial policies and procedures, resulting in inconsistent application and undocumented results that cannot be reported to regulators or buyers. In the human rights dimension, for example, they may have an awareness of the importance of preventing child labour, but no documented system for checking the age of workers. In the environmental dimension, there is little awareness of the need to measure greenhouse gas emissions and decarbonize the production process, for example, and to assess natural resource consumption. Governance is a weak area, with undeveloped board structures, informal anti-bribery policies, and a lack of ongoing due diligence processes to identify and address social and environmental risks.

The transition group has more established organizational structures and systems but faces challenges in effective implementation and adaptation to local circumstances. They have formal policies and procedures for human and labour rights, functioning Human Resource departments and reasonable working conditions, but implementation could be strengthened to deliver more consistent performance. They lack formal policies and systems for environmental risk however, and are not conducting risk assessments on their suppliers, materials, or production processes. They should improve resource-efficiency and explore circular production methods. In the governance dimension, there are gaps in anti-bribery policies, independent audit reviews, and formal procedures to ensure free, prior, and informed consent before acquiring land.

The tertiary group demonstrates well developed policies and procedures on human rights and labour issues, but implementation at farm level could be improved and environmental risk management strengthened. Greenhouse gas emissions risks are generally not being measured or managed, and there are significant risks related to water, waste and biodiversity that require greater attention. Many of the entities in this group hold recognized certifications and benefit from projects with international organizations. Some of the entities in the group recognized or negotiated with a trade union. This group performs relatively well in the governance dimension, but anti-bribery policies need to be formalized or strengthened, and many lack policies for securing free, prior, and informed consent before acquiring land.

# Conclusions

All three groups need to strengthen their due diligence procedures. They lack systems to collect data on the incoming and outgoing human rights and environmental risks related to their business activity, and they are therefore unable to prevent or mitigate those risks. This is the case with both the risks they buy-in when working with certain suppliers and materials, and the risks that flow from the way they produce and distribute their products.



<!-- PAGE: 16 -->

# Human Resource Management and Human Rights Issues

Human resource management is lacking in all groups, partly because of their MSME size and structure. Stronger human resource development and management would help to prevent human rights issues such as child and forced labour and equip employees to better mitigate environmental risks.

In the human rights dimension, freedom of association and living income or living wage require attention. The right to freedom of association needs to be clarified, especially in farming operations and cooperatives, and living wage or income targets need to be set with a plan to achieve them over a reasonable timeframe (five years, for example).

The groups all need to strengthen their due diligence systems to map their resource flows and identify the risks embedded in them. They also need clearer ESG policy objectives with designated managers, targets, and metrics. ESG performance could then be monitored and reported to buyers to qualify these MSME exporters as ‘due diligence ready.’

There is a strong need for capacity building to support the introduction and implementation of policies and procedures to identify and manage human rights and environmental risks, and to increase resource efficiency by collecting data on resource consumption.

# Case Study

The cohort from the pilot project implemented in Ghana covered the cocoa value chain from bean to bar. The ESG Readiness Assessment results were strongest in the social dimension, followed by governance, while preparedness in the environmental dimension was weaker.

Most respondents have yet to start collecting data on the human rights and environmental risks that may be embedded in the goods and services they purchase. Respondents also need to assess the risks they themselves may generate in their production and distribution activities. Pilot participants should define ESG-related goals and targets and develop systems to ensure they are properly implemented and monitored over time. Policies to ensure worker’s freedom of association should also be clarified and formalized.

In the environmental dimension, most of the entities need to strengthen their systems for documenting the origins of their production inputs and reducing the carbon footprint of their value chain.

The cohort performs relatively well in the governance dimension, but some areas for improvement remain regarding anti-bribery policies, and systems for securing free, prior, and informed consent before acquiring land.

Overall, there is a strong need for capacity building to support the introduction and implementation of policies and procedures to identify and manage human rights and environmental risks, and to increase resource efficiency and circular production systems.

Case Study

# Implementing Human Rights and Environmental Due Diligence with CLAC-Fairtrade Producer Organizations

To ensure that no one is left behind in accessing markets with mandatory human right and environment due diligence requirements, inclusive stakeholder engagement is key. This means that MSMEs and producer organizations need to be involved in identifying and prioritizing accompanying measures to mitigate potential barriers to business. CLAC-Fairtrade, representing around 800 small producer organizations across Latin America and the Caribbean, proved an ideal partner for the mHREDD pilots. Their mandate aims for producers to not only have a seat at the table, but to be at the centre of all the work, with producers co-owning the Fairtrade label and taking the lead on organizational activities.

Through the CLAC-Fairtrade -led pilots, four producer organizations in Peru, Dominican Republic, Brazil and Honduras implemented the six steps of Human Rights Due Diligence (HRDD) as per UNGP and OECD guidelines. The overall objectives of the pilots were:

1. To assess Implementation Requirements – understanding the time and resources required for coffee and cocoa producer organizations to implement HRDD within their regional context.
2. To develop Practical Tools – co-creating practical tools for and with producers to implement HREDD (in line with legislation like the EU CSDDD), tailored to context and available resources. This case study focuses on tool development.

The CLAC-Fairtrade technical team of human rights experts developed the tools for HREDD implementation, drawing on their extensive experience working with producer organizations across the region. The tools were designed to be accessible without internet, computers, or other technological resources, to enable for use in remote or rural settings with limited infrastructure. The language and format were also guided by popular education and Training of Trainer approaches, to promote understanding and peer-to-peer learning. The ultimate objective was to ensure that due diligence costs for producers do not further strain their living income situation.

Within the framework of the pilots, the newly developed tools were tested during the implementation of the 6-step due diligence process in each producer organization. To help producers understand the tools, use them correctly and provide feedback for improvement, the CLAC-Fairtrade technical team carried out workshops, trainings and follow up meetings. Tools created included:

- workshop slides and facilitation guides for the due diligence steps
- templates for HREDD planning, commitment, risk assessment, action plan and monitoring KPIs, communication plans, etc.

# LEVERAGING INVESTMENT AND FINANCE

# to Support Due Diligence and Accompanying Measures

Embedding effective due diligence capabilities into global value chains can be costly and complex, especially since those contexts in which due diligence is most needed and legally required often have least capacity to build and manage the requisite systems. Whilst there is some reassurance in the underlying logic of prioritization and continuous improvement within the UNGPs and the OECD Guidelines, many value chains are rooted in contexts of high endemic risk that offer little scope for a more progressive, selective approach. Leveraging co-investment and finance is a pre-requisite to enable the effective implementation and uptake of accompanying measures.

Costs associated with due diligence that fall to businesses and value chain actors include:

- Reskilling and capacity building for employees and business partners
- Designing, defining, and embedding policies and processes
- Enabling technologies
- Conducting specific assessments (e.g. risk assessments)
- Running and managing parallel support systems (e.g. for grievance mechanisms)
- Adequately remediating identified impacts
- Adjusting business operations generally to meet higher ESG standards and prevent negative impacts from occurring

There is currently a lack of reliable information on the real costs of implementing due diligence in different contexts. The following examples offer a sense of some of the cost implications in certain specific settings.

# What does it cost to do due diligence?

CLAC-Fairtrade has estimated the potential costs associated with establishing and implementing human rights and environmental due diligence systems faced specifically by smallholder producer associations. Notwithstanding significant variance depending on the size of the cooperative, the sector, the geography/context, the due diligence scope and the prevalent risks, CLAC-Fairtrade estimates an initial first year ‘launch’ cost of at least US$ 20,000 per cooperative (averaging 200 farmers per coop) covering:

1. Awareness-raising of producers/workers and socialization of due diligence within the cooperative
2. Conducting of a risk assessment and establishing a grievance mechanism
3. Development and implementation of a remediation action plan including associated policies (noting that this cost can vary enormously depending on the type and scale of remediation required)
4. Monitoring of progress and communication on impact

The International Cocoa Initiative’s (ICI) Child Labour Monitoring &#x26; Remediation System (CLMRS) is a supply-chain-integrated due diligence approach that actively identifies and addresses one salient human rights risk (child labour) in the West African cocoa supply chain on an ongoing basis. Costs vary across contexts, depending on the scale and scope of the system, the system’s maturity, the degree of mainstreaming and integration with other sustainability and due diligence efforts, and the remediation activities put in place to address any identified child labour. According to ICI, it can cost approximately US$ 50 per year, per farming household covered.

# Indicative cost ranges for outsourcing specific due diligence services or functions to specialist management consultancies may include:

- Staff training for generic due diligence awareness and specialist capability: US$ 50,000 - 150,000
- HQ-level human resource (HR) DD gap-assessment: US$ 75,000 - 250,000
- HQ-level human rights saliency assessment: US$ 50,000 - 100,000
- Ground-level human rights risk/impact assessment plus action plan: US$ 75,000 - 250,000

There is a clear and necessary role for investors in this space, not only in helping investee companies and value chain actors access the capital required to comply with due diligence requirements, but also to leverage those requirements in pursuit of positive social and environmental impact. The application of innovative finance to due diligence is a relatively nascent field of opportunity, but there is a growing body of financing approaches and instruments applied to broader ESG and sustainability goals that could prove highly relevant to the task of upscaling due diligence.

# KEY TAKEAWAYS &#x26; INSIGHTS from the Pilot

# A blueprint for making mHREDD work for all

Global value chains exhibit complex, imbalanced relationships, leaving MSMEs and smallholder producers vulnerable. Access to information, knowledge and adequate resources are critical to meet due diligence obligations and redressing imbalances.

An accompanying measure should prioritize the creation and dissemination of context-relevant awareness materials to address the mHREDD requirements effectively. Comprehensive communication is crucial for understanding the consequences and implications of distinct legislative frameworks on mHREDD. This includes addressing concerns, raising awareness, and dispelling misconceptions to support value chain operators and countries in harnessing opportunities effectively.

Defining and clearly communicating the role of voluntary sustainability schemes and certifications is essential within the broader strategy for mHREDD implementation.

Establishing impartial ‘inclusive communication spaces’ facilitates better dialogue and understanding among stakeholders in value chains, especially empowering underrepresented MSMEs and producers. These spaces are vital for fostering equitable transformation within value chains.

The implementation of pilots and accompanying measures is necessary to facilitate co-investment by all parties in value chains. This ensures a fair distribution of costs and value associated with due diligence implementation, risk mitigation, and capacity building.

Government agencies and industry associations can provide pre-competitive platforms for data collection and sharing to address imbalances and mitigate risks. Such platforms enable the sharing of responsibility and prevent the exclusion of data-deprived countries, producers, or MSMEs.

Stakeholders often lack access to information on harmonized and effective tools for supply chain management, risk assessment, and reporting – posing a significant challenge for CSDDD implementation.

# LIVING INCOME,

# a basic human right

- Living incomes and wages stand out as prominent human rights risks in the Readiness Assessments. Most surveyed entities lack a living wage or income target, along with a plan to achieve it.
- Addressing this issue could also mitigate risks of child and forced labour, informal work, and income inequality.
- The UNGPs, OECD and CSDDD specify that companies must ensure their business plans and purchasing practices contribute to living wages and living incomes along their chain of activities.
- This responsibility should not solely rest on individual producers and MSMEs but could be facilitated by sectoral organizations and companies offering multisourcing service integration engaging in pre-competitive collaboration and resource sharing.

# Stakeholder Roles, Capacity Building

# Responsibilities and Theories of Change

Ensure that all those working in value chains in a given country, from producer to exporter, have a voice and can work together to achieve collective fulfilment of mHREDD requirements. By doing this, more vulnerable actors like smallholder producers and MSMEs are not left behind. This will help:

- Each actor along the value chain to be held more accountable to one another and the sector as a whole by enabling them to operate more transparently, and by improving the way in which they run their operations.
- MSMEs and others operating in specific value chains to draft strategies (Theories of Change) that identify how they will seize the opportunities and address the risks the EU’s requirements present.
- Define metrics and standards that could be used to:
- Gauge the impact of the EU’s requirements on value chain actors.
- Help those actors operate more responsibly and accountably.
- Address negative impacts resulting from or related to their business operations.
- MSMEs in accessing new markets, technology, and finance with consideration to local context, risks, and opportunities.

# Support:

- MSMEs working in specific value chains in a given country to carry out the required human rights and environmental checks on their operations.
- Producers, traders, processors, buyers, and other value chain operators to acquire new skills to meet the requirements, whilst becoming more competitive and resilient in the process.
- Business support organizations and policymakers to develop new tools and services to help their members carry out the required due diligence.
- Value chains and their stakeholders to:
- Identify and prevent risks.
- Collect and report on risk management performance data.

# Research and Policy Guidance

Examine the challenges and opportunities arising from conducting due diligence into businesses’ performance in upholding human rights and environmental protection. Use of tools to gather trade intelligence and analysis of national and international markets can provide:

- Insights into relevant global value chain sustainability trends for MSMEs and policymakers in producing countries.
- Tailored advice for governments to develop policies that help value chain operators – including MSMEs and producers – to access EU and other international markets, to comply with the EU’s requirements on human rights and environmental protection, and to take advantage of new export opportunities.
